## In the book

The van is grinding up a forest track as the light goes, and Viraat is sweating at the wheel. Asked what he is so worried about, he answers with a list, and this is the name that comes first: "Banmanus! And pichhal peris, pishachas, kimpurushas, kinnars."

Behind his head the dark forest fills up with shapes, and the largest of them is a shaggy giant of a man standing between the trunks, pale-eyed, unmoving.

## In tradition

*Ban-manush* is simply "forest man" — *ban*, forest, and *manush*, human. He belongs to the hill folklore of northern and central India: a tall, hair-covered figure, enormously strong, glimpsed at the edge of cultivation and blamed for things that go missing. The Himalayan versions shade into the same body of belief that produced the yeti further north, and the pattern — a wild double of ourselves, living just past the last field — turns up wherever forest meets village.

## And yet

Viraat's list is answered immediately, and flatly: "Age-old legends, fabricated to scare children from straying into forests. Now used by Mahant to scare peasants from escaping."

That is the whole argument of this section in two sentences. The creature begins as a way of keeping children out of the trees, and ends as a way of keeping adults in the valley.
