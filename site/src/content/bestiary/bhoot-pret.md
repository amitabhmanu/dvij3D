## In the book

They get no picture, only places and a correction.

The Forest Officer's bungalow, abandoned and overgrown in the last of the light, is "a bhoot bungalow with a sinister look". The Kaal Bhairava temple floats on **Bhootnath** taal — the lake of the lord of bhoots — with vultures over the cremation ground beside it.

Then, on the night of the new moon, the yogini says the thing that puts them in their place: "Bhoots, prets prey on unsuspecting souls, not forlorn ones."

## In tradition

A **bhoot** is one of the dead who has not been settled: someone who died violently, or too young, or whose funeral rites were never properly done, and who therefore stays. The folk marks are well known — no shadow, a fear of iron and turmeric, and feet that point backwards.

A **pret** is at an earlier stage: the dead in the interval between the funeral and the rites that make them a *pitṛ*, an ancestor. A pret is hungry and unplaced by definition, which is the whole reason for the *shraddha* rites. The point of the ceremonies is to stop anyone having to be one.

## And yet

The yogini's sentence is not a warning, it is a limit. Bhoots and prets prey on *unsuspecting* souls — so if you know what is happening to you, they have nothing to work with. She says it to two people who are frightened in a forest at night, and it calms them down.

It is the most useful thing anyone in the book says about a monster: not that it does not exist, but that it only has the power you have not accounted for.
