## In the book

The last word of Viraat's list, and the last thing he says before the Professor cuts him off: "…pishachas, kimpurushas, **kinnars**."

He is naming them the way they are usually named — in the same breath as the kimpurushas, as though the two were one item. The art draws a single hybrid at the edge of that panel, horse-headed, and this site assigns it to the [kimpurusha](/bestiary/kimpurusha/); the kinnara gets no picture rather than a borrowed one.

## In tradition

*Kiṃ-nara*, "is it a man?" — the same question as *kiṃ-puruṣa*, which is why the two names are so hard to separate. What the kinnara does have is a distinct later career: it becomes, above all, a **musician**.

In Kubera's court on Mount Kailasa the kinnaras sing and play while the [gandharvas](/bestiary/gandharva/) make their own music, and the two are constantly confused with each other too. Carried into Buddhist tradition and across Southeast Asia, the kinnara changes body: human above and bird below, the *kinnari* of Thai, Burmese and Javanese art with her tail and wings, usually shown in a pair and taken as an emblem of devoted love. That bird-form is the image most people now have of the word, and it is the furthest thing from a horse-faced man on a forest road.

**A caution about the split.** The older Sanskrit sources treat *kiṃnara* and *kiṃpuruṣa* as synonyms — the Amarakosha lists them together with *turaṅgavadana*, "horse-faced." Accounts that separate them contradict one another about which one has the horse's head and which the human's. Two entries here follow the book's two words, not a settled distinction.

## And yet

The Professor's verdict covers the whole list without pausing over any of it: "Age-old legends, fabricated to scare children from straying into forests. Now used by Mahant to scare peasants from escaping."

A creature that spends most of its history singing at a god's court ends up, on this road, as one more reason not to leave the valley.
