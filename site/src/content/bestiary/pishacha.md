## In the book

Named in Viraat's list on the forest road — "Banmanus! And pichhal peris, pishachas, kimpurushas, kinnars" — and drawn behind him, crouching out of the dark with its mouth open.

The book comes back to the territory rather than the creature. The Kaal Bhairava temple stands over a cremation ground with vultures turning above it, and its devotees "practise extreme ritual worship. Challenge not their beliefs." That is where pishachas are supposed to be.

## In tradition

Pishachas are the flesh-eaters of the epics and Puranas: haunters of cremation grounds, crossroads and empty houses, blamed for madness, wasting illness and sudden fevers. In the usual ordering of malevolent beings they sit below the rakshasas — less powerful, more squalid, and more likely to be encountered by an ordinary person on an ordinary road at night.

The aghori practice the book keeps circling — worship carried out in the cremation ground, among exactly what everyone else avoids — takes the same ground seriously for the opposite reason.

## And yet

The Professor's answer covers this one too: legends "fabricated to scare children from straying into forests." And the one genuinely dangerous thing on that road turns out to be the road — the wrecked blue car at the roadside, which is where Dvij was found.
