## In the book

This one is in the art and not in the lettering. While the Professor explains the history of Shayani Ekadashi and Dvij holds his head, a vignette opens beside them: a golden figure reclining on a serpent couch on a milk-ocean, the hoods fanned above him, a lotus rising at his feet.

The caption names the festival, not the serpent. Shayani Ekadashi *is* the day Vishnu lies down to sleep, so the picture is doing the naming.

Later the parchment produces a clue "like a coiled serpent", and the coil turns out to mean something else entirely — the kundalini, coiled at the base of the spine. The book has two serpents and keeps them apart.

## In tradition

**Shesha** — "the remainder", what is left when everything else has been counted away — is the thousand-hooded serpent floating on the ocean of milk, with Vishnu asleep on his coils between one world-age and the next. His other name, **Ananta**, means endless. In the iconography his hoods arch over the god's head as a canopy, exactly as they are drawn here.

## And yet

Nothing in the story asks anyone to believe in him. He appears as an illustration inside an explanation — a picture of what a festival is *about*, in a book whose Professor is always explaining what things are about. The serpent that matters to the plot is a diagram of a spine.
