At the foundation ceremony Dvij watches an event billed as "ask the expert": a man sits in the middle of a ring of questioners, who throw challenges at him from every side, and he answers each one in composed verse without losing track of any of them. The Professor, delighted, supplies the name.

> **Avadhan.** That's what this is called. Many people ask questions. Expert has to remember all of them, then respond to each in verses following rules of *rasa* and *alankara*.

**Avadhāna** means attention, or concentration. In the performance, several questioners (*pṛcchaka*) each set the *avadhāni* a separate task — compose in a given metre, answer a riddle, complete a verse, count the bells of a ringing bell — and put them in fragments, out of order, interleaved with deliberate distraction. The avadhani holds every thread, returns to each in turn, and delivers the finished answers in order at the end. The genre is strongest in Telugu and Sanskrit traditions and is still performed.

The size of the ring names the feat: **aṣṭāvadhāna** is eight questioners, **śatāvadhāna** a hundred, and **sahasrāvadhāna** a thousand. The panel in the book draws a ring of ten.

One of the standard questioners has no question at all. The *apraṣṭuta-prasaṅga* simply interrupts — irrelevant, needling, timed to break concentration — and the avadhani must field him too. The Professor gets the same treatment on the same page, when the dignitaries turn round and hush him mid-explanation.

What Dvij takes away is not the showmanship but the method behind it, which is [the recitation patterns](/codex/vedic-pathas/) the Professor names in the next balloon. Three books later it gets him out of a cave.

> Professor explained their memorization technique to me. I tried it on a shloka and it worked.
