The Nagas run underneath this book from the first chapter to the last third, always spoken about and never quite met.

The cook warns the newcomer off the forest with them: "There are wild animals! **Nagas** in the forests, and Yakshas hide in the trees. Nobody dares, sahib." The Professor names Kamakhya as "a **Naga tribal** — fiercely proud, wary of strangers." Kurup, much later, calls the Himapan forest "home of the **violent Nagas**" — and then admits that the thing in that forest even the Nagas fear is a tiger.

The fullest account is the Professor's, and it is the one the art illustrates, in smoky frames behind him: tribesmen with spears, uniformed settlers with rifles, women hiding their faces, a burning village, a wounded tiger.

> The Nagas are ardent aghori followers. They have suffered much. The men butchered and women abused by centuries of civilized settlers. That tribe is like a wounded tiger eager to avenge.

**Not the serpent.** The Sanskrit *nāga*, the serpent-being, is a different word with a different history, and nothing in this book connects the two. The valley also has a **Naga hill**, whose soft stone the temple carvers used. Three unrelated things wearing one spelling — which is why the [Bestiary](/bestiary/) has no naga entry (§11.5).

## Outside the book

The **Naga** are not one people but many: some dozens of groups — Angami, Ao, Sema, Konyak, Lotha, Tangkhul and others — living across Nagaland, Manipur, Arunachal Pradesh and Assam, and over the border into Myanmar, speaking different Tibeto-Burman languages and historically organised as independent villages rather than a single state. "Naga" is an umbrella name applied from outside; its origin is disputed and none of the proposed derivations involves serpents.

The ceremonial dress in the drawing is the part outsiders always recorded: hornbill feathers, the broad-bladed *dao*, a shield. Colonial ethnography fixed on headhunting and rarely on much else, and the illustrated warrior standing alone against a blank page is itself a product of that way of looking. The weaving, the woodcarving, the morung, the village republics and the oral literature got far less attention.

Most Naga today are Christian, largely Baptist, following missions from the late nineteenth century onwards. The political conflict over Naga sovereignty has run since 1947 and is not settled.

## What the book does with them

Two things, and they sit awkwardly together, which is worth noticing rather than smoothing over.

The Professor's speech is on their side. It names the dispossession squarely and puts the blame on "civilized settlers" — the quotation marks are the book's own irony. The art backs him: the rifles are on the settlers' side of the frame.

But the valley's Nagas are also the story's off-stage menace, the reason nobody goes into the forest, and Kurup's "violent Nagas" is exactly the stereotype the Professor had just finished dismantling. And the **aghori** connection is the novel's invention: it belongs to the valley's Shaiva-tantric world, not to the real Naga peoples, whose own traditions and later Christianity have nothing to do with it.

Read the entry as the book's fiction first, with the real peoples standing behind it, borrowed for a name and a silhouette.
