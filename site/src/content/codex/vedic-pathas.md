The Professor names the method in one breath, and the art puts the chart up beside him:

> **Techniques of memorization!** The entire Vedas handed down orally through millenniums! Students learned forward sequence, backward, skipping syllables — remember it forever!

The chart he is holding reads *1. Forward sequence · 2. Backward sequence · 3. Skipping syllables*. Those are the **pāṭhas**: fixed schemes for reciting a text in permuted order, each one harder than the last, learnt on top of the plain recitation rather than instead of it.

| Pāṭha | Over words 1 2 3 4 … |
|---|---|
| *saṃhitā* | the line as it is spoken, run together |
| *pada* | 1 · 2 · 3 · 4 — each word alone, sandhi undone |
| *krama* | 1‑2, 2‑3, 3‑4 |
| *jaṭā* | 1‑2 2‑1 1‑2, 2‑3 3‑2 2‑3 |
| *ghana* | 1‑2 2‑1 1‑2‑3 3‑2‑1 1‑2‑3, 2‑3 3‑2 2‑3‑4 4‑3‑2 2‑3‑4 |

A reciter who has mastered the last of them earns the title *ghanapāṭhin*.

**Why it works.** The point is not repetition — it is that every word is recited inside several different neighbourhoods, forwards and backwards, next to different partners. A syllable that drifts in one pass contradicts itself in the others, and the error is audible rather than silent. The pattern is redundancy deliberately built in so that mistakes cannot propagate: an error-correcting code, arrived at for a text nobody was allowed to write down, a very long time before anyone wrote a checksum.

It is why the *shrauta* texts could be, as the book puts it, "preserved by oral recitation" across millennia with so little drift, and why the udgatrus' Samaveda chanting is treated in Book 5 as something more exacting than singing.
