## In the book

Fourth on Viraat's list as the van climbs into the dark: "Banmanus! And pichhal peris, pishachas, kimpurushas, kinnars." At the right-hand edge of the forest behind him the art draws one — a horse's head on a man's body, gold chains at the throat, a sash across the chest, turned to watch the van go past.

It is the only one of the five on that list drawn clearly enough to identify by species.

## In tradition

The name is a question used as a name: *kiṃ-puruṣa*, "is it a man?" — a double-take at something that almost passes for human. The classic form is the one the panel draws, a human body under a horse's head, which is why the Amarakosha lists *turaṅgavadana*, "horse-faced," among its synonyms.

Puranic geography gives the kimpurushas a country of their own: **Kimpuruṣa-varṣa**, one of the regions of Jambudvipa, lying between the Himalaya and Hemakuta — the mountains to the north, which is roughly the direction the van is driving. They belong, with the yakshas and the [kinnaras](/bestiary/kinnara/), to **Kubera's** retinue.

**A caution about the split.** *Kimpuruṣa* and *kiṃnara* are the same question asked twice — *kiṃ-nara* also means "is it a man?" — and the older sources use them interchangeably; the Amarakosha simply lists them as synonyms. Later tradition does try to prise them apart, but the accounts disagree about which gets which body. Treat the division on this site as a convenience, not a finding.

## And yet

Same page, same answer: legends "fabricated to scare children from straying into forests. Now used by Mahant to scare peasants from escaping." The Professor does not distinguish between the creature whose name is a question in Sanskrit and the one that eats people. They are all doing the same job in the valley.
