## In the book

They flank the steps of the Kaal Bhairava temple, and the caption names them and explains them in one breath: "Ferocious vyalas flanked the steps — composite creatures controlling cosmic energy." The pair drawn there are *gaja-vyalas*, elephant-headed, trunks up, sitting above Dvij as he looks up the stairs.

The book draws a second kind without naming it. At the temple in Book 3 a guardian rears on a pillar and gets a full panel to itself in close-up: lion-faced, horned, fangs bared, a heavy mane of carved curls. Same creature, different head.

## In tradition

The *vyala* (in the south, *yali*) is one of the standard beasts of Indian temple architecture, on pillars, plinths and door jambs from the Chalukyan and Hoysala temples through the great Dravidian complexes. It is deliberately a hybrid — the body is a lion's, and the head may be a lion's, an elephant's, a horse's or a makara's, giving *simha-vyala*, *gaja-vyala*, *ashva-vyala* and so on. Guardian figures at a boundary, they are usually read as the wild energies of the world held at the threshold rather than let inside: powerful, and placed exactly where the building needs power to stop.

## And yet

The book's gloss — "controlling cosmic energy" — is the valley's way of talking, not an art historian's. The Professor's own reading of the same temples, a few pages later, is structural: proportion, the tower measured against a tree, the kalasha buried under the sanctum matching the roots. Two explanations of one building, and the book puts them side by side without choosing.
