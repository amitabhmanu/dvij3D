## In the book

The cook warns the newcomer about the forest: "There are wild animals! Nagas in the forests, and Yakshas hide in the trees. Nobody dares, sahib." Behind him hangs a painted forest scene, and in the branches sit two crowned, bejewelled figures, watching — with a tiger and a lion below them.

It is the first creature named in the book, on the page where the Mahant is deciding what to do with the wounded man he has been handed.

## In tradition

Yakshas are spirits of trees, groves, pools and buried treasure, and among the oldest divine figures in Indian art — the great Parkham yaksha and the Didarganj yakshi are centuries older than most temple sculpture. They serve **Kubera**, lord of wealth and guardian of the north, which is why they are so often shown crowned and loaded with jewellery: they hold what is buried. Their female counterparts, *yakshis*, stand at the gateways of Sanchi and Bharhut with an arm around a tree branch.

They are not evil, and not safe. A yaksha guards, and what it guards it may keep.

## And yet

Look at who is speaking, and to whom. The cook says nobody dares go into the forest; the next thing the book shows is the Mahant, in his pavilion at the edge of the cleared field, explaining that the wounded man is "a fool who dropped into my lap like a blessing". Seven pages later the Professor says it outright: the forest stories are "now used by Mahant to scare peasants from escaping."

A spirit in the trees keeps people where they are. That is the use it is being put to.
